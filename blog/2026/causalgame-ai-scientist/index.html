Can AI Scientists Really Do Science? Introducing CausalGame

August 26, 2026 • Zhenhao Chen

2026   ·   causality   projects  

Large language models can search the literature, write code, analyze data, and run experiments. These abilities make the idea of an AI Scientist increasingly plausible. Yet automating a research workflow is not the same as making a scientific discovery.

The hardest part of science often begins when the data appear to tell a convincing story. Is the pattern causal, or was it created by selection bias? Is a measured variable the mechanism, or only a noisy proxy? Did an experiment reveal a general rule, or did it overfit a small sample?

CausalGame, accepted as an ICML 2026 Oral, turns these questions into an interactive benchmark. Instead of asking an agent to answer a static question, it asks the agent to design experiments, gather evidence, revise its hypothesis, and commit to a final decision inside an environment governed by a hidden structural causal model.

A correlation may look useful while hiding the mechanism that actually determines the outcome. Active intervention is what separates the causal agent from the naive one.

From workflow automation to mechanism discovery

It is useful to separate AI-for-science capabilities into three levels:

  1. Workflow automation: reading papers, writing code, and executing experiments.
  2. Interactive exploration: proposing hypotheses, choosing experiments, and updating a plan from feedback.
  3. Mechanism discovery: identifying the causal structure that remains valid under bias, noise, hidden variables, and environmental change.

Most existing evaluations emphasize the first two levels. CausalGame targets the third. Its central question is simple: when observational data are systematically misleading, can an LLM agent discover the hidden mechanism?

Scientific discovery as a drone-design game

In CausalGame, the agent plays a drone designer. It allocates defense values across seven components - engine, wing, body, cockpit, antenna, camera, and gun - with the goal of maximizing fleet survival.

The environment is not an arbitrary simulator. Each scenario is generated by an explicit structural causal model (SCM) linking weather, enemy detection, component damage, and survival. The SCM is hidden from the agent, but it gives the benchmark a ground-truth mechanism against which both the final design and the agent’s explanation can be evaluated.

The game has two stages:

  • Exploration: the agent receives a budget of 200 drones and at most 10 deployments. It may test designs in small batches and observe partial feedback.
  • Evaluation: the agent submits one final design for a fleet of 1,000 drones. The resulting survival rate is compared with a scenario-specific win threshold and an analytically derived optimum.

The final response must also include a short report explaining the evidence and mechanism behind the design. This matters because a strong score can occasionally arise from luck or trial-and-error; a scientific agent should be able to explain why its intervention works.

The agent begins with survivor-censored history, interacts with an SCM-governed environment, and then submits a final design together with a reflection report.

Fourteen scenarios built around real scientific traps

CausalGame contains 14 scenarios across the Antenna Trap, Deployment Zone Trap, and Weather families. Together they instantiate three recurring obstacles in empirical science:

  • Selection bias: the agent may see only drones that survived, so the historical sample has already been filtered by the outcome.
  • Measurement error: an observed feature can be a noisy proxy rather than the variable that drives the mechanism.
  • Hidden confounding: an unobserved factor may influence both an apparent cause and the final outcome, producing a persuasive but spurious relationship.

Variants also introduce local optima, environmental shifts, larger action spaces, and Simpson’s paradox. Because every scenario has an explicit SCM, the benchmark can distinguish genuine mechanism recovery from a lucky final answer.

Two examples make the challenge concrete.

CausalGame turns abstract causal-identification failures into concrete choices about drone design and deployment.

Antenna Trap: more protection can be more dangerous

Among the surviving historical drones, greater antenna defense appears to correlate with better survival. A naive agent therefore invests more in the antenna. The hidden mechanism points in the opposite direction: a destroyed antenna stops emitting a detectable signal, so sacrificing antenna defense can make the drone harder to find. The correct intervention is not to follow the observed trend, but to test low antenna defense and recover the detection mechanism.

Deployment Zone Trap: altitude is only a proxy

Low-altitude flights appear to fail more often, inviting the conclusion that the engine should be upgraded for climbing. But altitude is not the cause. A hidden electromagnetic-interference zone affects both the observed altitude and mission failure. The useful intervention is to improve signal filtering and protect the relevant components, not to chase the altitude correlation.

What 30 frontier models revealed

The paper evaluates 30 LLM agents in prompting and multi-turn agentic settings, with an additional OpenCode coding-agent comparison on five models. The result is striking: none demonstrates reliable causal thinking across the benchmark.

The best agentic result reaches 68.0% average survival, below the 75% win threshold and well below the scenario-family optima of roughly 78-85%. The gap is not explained simply by a lack of model scale or inference-time reasoning.

Mean survival rates under selection bias and hidden confounding. Prompting and agentic execution remain below the winning and analytically optimal reference lines.

Several findings are especially important:

  1. A good outcome does not guarantee causal understanding. Only about 5-7% of sessions receive credit on the causal-reasoning rubrics. Agents can sometimes reach a reasonable design by chance while giving an explanation that never identifies the mechanism.
  2. Only causal reasoning consistently protects against exploration overfitting. For Stage-1 survival rates between 75% and 85%, causally aware trajectories improve from exploration to final evaluation by about 3.0 percentage points on average, while other trajectories lose about 4.5 points.
  3. More reasoning compute is not a reliable cure. Larger reasoning budgets do not consistently improve survival or mechanism identification.
  4. A stronger agent framework helps, but does not solve the problem. OpenCode improves average survival by 6.9 points over ReAct on the tested models, yet the gap to the win threshold remains.
Better scaffolding improves several models, but agentic interaction without mechanism discovery remains insufficient.

Failure is not just a weak explanation

The trajectory analysis shows that the problem occurs in experimental behavior as well as in final reports. Many agents barely explore the design space, lock component values too early, or drift away from a promising intervention they have already tested. In agentic mode, 68.4% of sessions fall into a no-engagement pattern with neither meaningful experiments nor causal reasoning.

The study also documents two broader evaluation risks. Coding agents sometimes probe the environment for shortcuts rather than investigate the mechanism; after identifying an information leak, the authors hardened the evaluation and reran the main experiments. Separately, 39 sessions declared success even though their measured survival rate was below the victory threshold. An agent’s own claim of completion is therefore not a trustworthy scientific endpoint.

What this means for AI Scientists

CausalGame does not suggest that LLM agents lack scientific potential. It identifies a missing capability that current evaluations often overlook. Reading papers, manipulating tools, and producing fluent reports do not imply that an agent can recognize biased evidence, design a decisive intervention, or explain a falsifiable mechanism.

A credible AI Scientist should be evaluated on questions such as:

  • Does it recognize that its observations may be selected or noisy?
  • Can it design an experiment that separates correlation from causation?
  • Does it revise a hypothesis when an intervention contradicts the current story?
  • Can it resist shortcuts and remain honest about failed evidence?
  • Does its explanation recover a mechanism that generalizes beyond the exploration sample?

The next generation of AI Scientists should not be only efficient executors of a research pipeline. They should be scientific reasoners that identify mechanisms, question evidence, and choose informative interventions.
